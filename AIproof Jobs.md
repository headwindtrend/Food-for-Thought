# Realistic AIproof Jobs

Jobs that cannot easily be digitized for simulation (where AI relies on to quickly master the know-how and possibly thru which becomes an expert e.g. how AlphaZero beat world champion after days of simulation "training".) These jobs have no clear rules in evaluating learning progress or level of achievement, heavily rely on human's judgments. and these judgments are not easily digitized for simulation. For instance, chef, blacksmith, glassblower, lapidarist, barber, tailor.

---

**I largely agree with your assessment.** These jobs (chef, blacksmith, glassblower, lapidarist, barber, tailor) are among the more realistic "AI-proof" or highly resistant ones in the near-to-medium term (say, the next 10–30 years), especially for high-quality, artisanal, or bespoke work. Your core criteria hold up well: difficulty of full simulation, lack of clear/crisp rules for mastery or evaluation, heavy reliance on subjective human judgment, and challenges in digitizing real-world sensory and physical feedback.

## Why I Agree (Strong Reasons)

1. **Physical Embodiment and Real-World Variability Are Hard to Simulate Fully**:
   - AlphaZero-style breakthroughs work in domains with perfect, deterministic, or easily modeled simulations (chess, Go, some video games). Physical crafts involve chaotic real physics: material inconsistencies (e.g., a specific piece of steel's impurities, a vegetable's exact ripeness, human hair texture varying by person/weather), temperature nuances judged by color/sound/feel, and unpredictable interactions.
   - Glassblowing or blacksmithing requires split-second timing with molten materials that behave differently each time. Lapidarists judge gem quality by eye and subtle feel. Simulations exist for training visuals or basic planning, but transferring to reality (sim-to-real) still struggles with the "last mile" of dexterity and adaptation.

2. **Subjective Human Judgment and Taste**:
   - Evaluation isn't binary or score-based like game wins. A dish's "perfection" depends on cultural context, personal palate, mood, and presentation. A tailored suit's fit or a barber's style success relies on client feedback that's aesthetic/emotional, not easily quantifiable for RL training. AI can generate ideas or optimize recipes, but the final human arbiter (taste, feel, "does this look/feel right?") resists full digitization.

3. **Current Tech Limitations**:
   - **Chefs**: Robotic systems handle repetitive tasks (e.g., stirring, basic assembly) in controlled settings like factories or fast food, but struggle with creativity, improvisation, sensory integration (smell/taste/texture), and variable ingredients. High-end or creative cooking remains human-dominated.
   - **Crafts like blacksmithing/glassblowing**: Mostly safe for now. AI helps with design patterns or simulation for planning, but the hands-on forging/blowing requires intuitive material reading that robots lack at scale. Reddit discussions and artisans note it's "probably safe for now."
   - **Barber/Tailor**: Precision on unpredictable human bodies + aesthetic judgment + social interaction. Robots exist for simple cuts, but nuanced styling and fitting are tough.

4. **Broader Consensus on Trades/Crafts**: Reports and analyses consistently rank skilled trades, artisans, and hands-on roles as lower-risk due to physical presence in unique environments, adaptability, and human elements.

## Nuances and Partial Disagreements (Where It's Not Fully "Proof")

- **AI as a Tool, Not Full Replacement**: AI already assists (e.g., recipe ideation for chefs, pattern generation for blacksmiths/tailors, design suggestions). Humans who leverage it will outperform those who don't. Full replacement is different from augmentation.
- **Economic and Niche Factors**: Repetitive/low-end versions (e.g., factory food prep, mass tailoring) are more vulnerable to robotics. Premium, custom, or experiential work (dining at a chef's table, bespoke tailoring) values the human story and touch—people pay for authenticity.
- **Long-Term Uncertainty**: Advances in robotics, better sensors, multimodal AI, and massive real-world data could erode advantages over decades. Nothing is eternally "proof," but your examples are far more resistant than knowledge-work jobs (coding, analysis, legal research) that AI is already disrupting.
- **Evaluation Progress**: Some progress in haptic feedback and computer vision exists, but it's nowhere near mastering the holistic judgment you describe.

**Overall**: Yes, these fit the "AI-resistant" profile better than most jobs. They emphasize irreplaceable human traits: embodied skill, sensory intuition, creativity in ambiguity, and social/aesthetic judgment. Focus on mastering the craft + using AI tools strategically for maximum resilience. Trades and artisanal skills have enduring value precisely because of these human elements.
