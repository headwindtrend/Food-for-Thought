# From saving Juliet, to breaking determinism, to what is freedom

E.T. stared at the television screen.

Juliet had taken the potion.

Romeo believed she was dead.

The tragedy was unfolding exactly as it always had.

"No..." E.T. whispered.

His long neck drooped.

His glowing finger dimmed.

To E.T., Elliott, and Michael, the story was not merely entertainment. They had always assumed that *Romeo and Juliet*, like many old plays, was based on real events from long ago. The actors were actors, of course, but the lovers themselves were people who had once lived.

And now, from their perspective, history was heading toward disaster.

"Need save," E.T. murmured.

Michael rolled his eyes.

"E.T., that happened centuries ago."

E.T.'s eyes suddenly widened.

Then he smiled.

A very dangerous smile.

"Oh no," said Elliott. "I know that face."

E.T. pointed excitedly toward the strange machine sitting in the corner of the garage.

His time machine.

Well, "time machine" was probably the wrong term.

It couldn't send people into the past.

It couldn't even move information into the past.

What it could do was stranger.

It could rewind the entire causal chain to a previous state and let history run again.

Like resetting a simulation.

Like loading an old save file.

Except nobody remembered the previous run.

History simply happened again.

"Wait," said Elliott. "You want to rewind history and save Juliet?"

E.T. nodded vigorously.

Michael frowned.

"How would that help?"

E.T. pointed at a physics textbook lying nearby.

"Quantum randomness."

"What?"

"Quantum randomness."

E.T. tapped the book.

"If quantum events truly random... then rerun enough times... eventually different outcome."

Michael thought for a moment.

Then his face brightened.

Then it darkened again.

"Dang."

E.T. tilted his head.

"Dang?"

"How would we know when the lucky run happens?"

E.T. froze.

His finger stopped glowing.

A full ten seconds passed.

Then he sighed.

"Dang."

The three sat in silence.

A brilliant plan had collided with a devastating practical problem.

If a million reruns were needed, nobody would know which run was the millionth.

Each run would feel like the first.

Every failure would be forgotten.

Every success would be invisible until it happened.

The machine suddenly seemed much less magical.

But the conversation had already drifted somewhere unexpected.

They had started by asking whether Juliet could be saved.

Now they were asking what it would even mean for history to unfold differently.

And that question led naturally to another:

If history were rerun, would people make different choices?

---

A while later Michael asked exactly that.

"Actually... if we kept rerunning history, would people make different choices?"

Elliott shrugged.

"Maybe."

E.T. looked uncertain.

Michael continued.

"Suppose we rewind to this exact moment."

He pointed around the room.

"Same garage. Same conversation. Same people."

E.T. nodded.

"Same everything?"

"Yes."

"Then why would I say anything different?"

E.T. thought about it.

Michael continued.

"If every atom in my brain is arranged exactly the same way, wouldn't I think exactly the same thoughts?"

Michael picked up a pencil from the table.

"Suppose I decide to put this pencil down."

He placed it on the table.

"If history is deterministic, then I put it down because of who I am, what I know, what I want, and what I'm thinking."

He paused.

"Maybe that sounds like I'm trapped."

Elliott scratched his head.

"Isn't it like a play? We're just acting out a story, everything is predetermined. no freedom."

E.T. walked in circles.

It was one of the habits he had when thinking.

Finally he stopped.

"Unless it's something else which makes decisions for you."

Elliott looked puzzled.

"What do you mean?"

E.T. continued.

"Randomness."

E.T. grabbed two toy blocks and placed them on the table.

"Left block. Right block."

He pointed at Elliott.

"You choose."

Elliott pointed.

"Left."

E.T. shook his head.

"No. Coin choose."

He flipped a coin.

Right.

"There."

Elliott frowned.

"That's not my choice."

E.T. pointed triumphantly.

"It breaks determinism, why not?"

"Because the coin decided."

Michael agreed with Elliott.

"If I let the coin decide for me whether to put the pencil down, then whose choice is it? The coin's. And that's what feels less free."

---

The discussion continued late into the night.

What had begun as an attempt to rescue Juliet had become something else entirely.

The original question was simple:

Could history have gone differently?

But hidden inside it was a deeper question:

Could *we* have gone differently?

Suppose the universe were perfectly deterministic.

Then every decision would emerge from:

* memories,
* beliefs,
* desires,
* reasoning,
* personality.

Rewind history.

The same person would make the same choice.

Again.

And again.

And again.

At first this sounded depressing.

Like a prison.

Like a script.

Like a puppet show.

After all, many people instinctively imagine freedom as the ability to have done otherwise.

If Romeo always chooses the same thing.

If Juliet always chooses the same thing.

If Elliott always chooses the same thing.

Then where is freedom?

---

But then E.T. asked another question.

"If not that... then what?"

Silence.

"If not memories... what choose?"

Silence.

"If not beliefs... what choose?"

Silence.

"If not goals... what choose?"

Silence.

Finally Michael answered.

"Randomness?"

E.T. immediately flipped the coin again.

The room burst into laughter.

---

The more they talked, the stranger freedom became.

At first freedom seemed to mean:

Being free from causes.

But every attempt to remove causes made freedom look worse.

Remove values.

Remove goals.

Remove memories.

Remove reasoning.

Replace them with randomness.

The result no longer looked free.

It looked arbitrary.

Yet there was an important distinction.

A decision influenced by quantum randomness somewhere inside your brain is not necessarily the same thing as a decision made by a coin.

If Michael freely decides to throw the pencil, then the action still feels like *his* action, regardless of whether quantum events played some role in producing that decision.

The problem is not randomness itself.

The problem is outsourcing the decision.

When the coin decides, the choice is no longer yours.

It belongs to the coin.

And that is precisely why it feels less free.

And suddenly the original mission to save Juliet looked different.

Suppose E.T. reran history a billion times.

Suppose one run eventually produced a different outcome.

What would that mean?

Would Juliet be saved because she freely chose differently?

Or because some microscopic random event nudged history onto another path?

The more they examined the idea, the less obvious it became that randomness was the kind of freedom they had been looking for.

---

Near midnight E.T. sat quietly.

Then he pointed at Elliott.

"You choose."

Elliott nodded.

"Yes."

"You think."

"Yes."

"You care."

"Yes."

"You imagine future."

"Yes."

"You decide."

"Yes."

E.T. smiled.

"Then maybe freedom not absence of causal chain."

Elliott frowned.

"What do you mean?"

E.T. pointed to a bicycle leaning against the wall.

"If bicycle part of motion..."

Then he pointed to Elliott.

"...and Elliott part of causal chain..."

Then he pointed to his own chest.

"...and E.T. part of causal chain..."

Finally he pointed upward, as if tracing an invisible path through time.

"Maybe freedom is not outside causal chain."

Nobody spoke.

Even Michael remained silent.

E.T. finished softly:

"Maybe freedom is being part of causal chain."

---

The room grew quiet.

Juliet was still doomed.

The reruns, if they happened at all, remained invisible.

The universe was no easier to understand than before.

Yet something had changed.

They had begun the evening wanting to save Juliet.

They ended it wondering what freedom itself could possibly mean.

The idea that freedom required escaping causality had begun to look suspicious.

After all, whenever they tried to imagine a choice detached from reasons, values, memories, and goals, the result did not feel more free.

It felt less.

The counterintuitive possibility was this:

The things that seem to threaten freedom may be exactly the things that create it.

A choice caused by your beliefs, your character, your hopes, and your understanding may be more recognizably *yours* than a choice produced by a cosmic coin flip.

And perhaps the deepest confusion comes from treating all forms of indeterminism as if they were the same.

If quantum randomness helps shape what *you* ultimately decide, the resulting action can still feel fully yours.

But if the decision is handed over to a coin, a die, or some external randomizer, freedom seems to disappear rather than increase.

The coin-flip solution looks, at first glance, like a way to escape determinism.

Yet it achieves that escape by replacing *your* decision with something else's.

Perhaps the deepest lesson hidden inside the question of determinism is that freedom may not be the power to stand outside the causal order.

It may be the power of a conscious being to participate in it.

What exactly is freedom remains uncertain. It's just doesn't seem like the opposite of determinism as we intuitively presume though.
