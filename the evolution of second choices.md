# The Evolution of Second Choices

Why did humans become human?

The obvious answer is our brain. Yet that merely postpones the question.

## Nature usually solves problems with better hardware

Look at successful predators.

An eagle has extraordinary eyesight.

A wolf has an incredible sense of smell.

A shark detects tiny electrical signals.

A bat uses echolocation.

A lion possesses explosive speed and powerful claws.

Evolution often solves survival problems by building better hardware.

Humans are peculiar.

We are not especially fast.

We are not especially strong.

Our teeth are unimpressive.

Our claws are practically nonexistent.

Our sense of smell is mediocre.

Our hearing is ordinary.

Our night vision is poor.

From a physical perspective, we are strangely under-equipped.

And yet we became one of the most successful hunters on Earth.

## The irony of lacking

Our greatest strength may have begun as a weakness.

Animals with extraordinary noses can smell.

Animals with extraordinary speed can sprint.

Animals with powerful claws can overpower.

Humans often had to think.

## The anatomy diversion

Many animals possess sophisticated brains. Chimpanzees form alliances. Dolphins cooperate in complex social groups. Crows solve puzzles. Octopuses display remarkable problem-solving abilities. Evolution had already invented learning long before humans appeared.

So perhaps humans were not born with a fundamentally different architecture. Perhaps our ancestors began with roughly the same machinery for learning as many other intelligent animals.

In fact, I incline to assume, specifically, human ancestors were physiologically identical to other apes, say, our closest relatives, the chimpanzees. Why did our lineage develop such extraordinary cognitive abilities while chimpanzees followed a different path?

Many explanations have been proposed: language, fire, meat, tools, cooperation, culture. Each undoubtedly played an important role. But perhaps they were not the first domino. Perhaps they were consequences of something more fundamental.

I would like to propose a different way of looking at the problem.

Instead of asking what made humans stronger, smarter, or more capable, ask a simpler question:

**What if our ancestors simply lost their first choice?**

## Nature rarely changes successful strategies

Evolution is conservative.

If a strategy works well, there is little reason to replace it.

Chimpanzees are remarkably successful animals. Living in forests, they climb efficiently, gather fruit, occasionally hunt, and cooperate socially. Their way of life has worked for millions of years.

If resources remain abundant and the environment remains suitable, why change?

The interesting question is not why chimpanzees did not become human.

The interesting question is why one ape lineage stopped living like chimpanzees.

## When the old way stops working

Imagine Africa gradually changing.

Forests become patchier.

Open woodland and grassland spread.

Food becomes less predictable.

Competition for the remaining forests intensifies.

Some populations continue living where the old strategy still works.

Others cannot.

Whether because they lost competition, occupied less favorable territory, or simply found themselves in changing environments, they face a difficult reality:

The old solution is no longer good enough.

Evolution does not reward them with a better body overnight.

Instead, it presents a problem.

Survive anyway.

## Second choices

Leaving the familiar forest is not an improvement.

It is a second choice.

Walking farther to find food is a second choice.

Searching larger territories is a second choice.

Eating unfamiliar foods is a second choice.

Opportunistically hunting animals is a second choice.

None of these decisions need to be perfect.

They only need to work slightly better than starving.

This distinction matters.

As the adaptation, traveling longer and longer distances gradually changed their anatomy.

* Efficient sweating
* Relatively hairless skin
* Long legs
* An upright posture

Longer distance travel foraging might have implied that hunting perhaps therefore became more important in food supply.

Throwing stones at the prey was probably their early hunting weapon/skill.

As the adaptation, stones throwing gradually changed their anatomy.

* A very mobile shoulder
* A long waist that rotates
* Elastic tendons storing energy
* Relatively longer legs and narrower pelvis allowing trunk rotation
* Fine motor control

Many discussions of human evolution begin with sophisticated behaviors such as persistence hunting or complex toolmaking. But such behaviors probably did not appear fully formed.

Evolution almost never works that way.

Instead, it accumulates countless small improvements, each solving the problems created by the previous solution.

## The accidental hunter

Imagine early humans crossing open country in search of food.

They encounter animals.

Not because they are hunting them.

Simply because both inhabit the same landscape.

Sometimes an opportunity appears.

A young antelope.

An injured animal.

One separated from the herd.

A stone is thrown.

Perhaps the animal escapes.

Perhaps it leaves blood.

Perhaps it leaves footprints.

Now the hunt is unfinished.

A new problem has been created.

If the hunter can follow the clues, dinner is still possible.

Tracking is no longer a curiosity.

It becomes valuable.

## Reading invisible stories

Humans are physically unremarkable hunters.

We are slower than many prey.

We have no claws.

No fangs.

No exceptional sense of smell.

No remarkable night vision.

Yet we possess one unusual physical advantage.

We are exceptional endurance walkers and runners.

But endurance alone cannot catch prey.

Sooner or later, the animal disappears beyond a hill.

Now success depends on something entirely different.

A broken twig.

A bent blade of grass.

A faint footprint.

Disturbed soil.

None of these is the prey.

They are evidence.

The hunter must reconstruct an invisible story from visible traces.

Where did the animal go?

How long ago?

Is it injured?

Will it seek water?

Every clue eliminates possibilities.

Tracking is not simply another hunting technique.

It is inference.

It is reasoning from incomplete information.

It is solving a mystery written on the ground.

## When learning becomes the better adaptation

Many animals evolved specialized hardware.

Eagles evolved extraordinary eyesight.

Wolves evolved extraordinary smell.

Lions evolved speed and claws.

Humans appear to have followed a different path.

Instead of investing only in specialized anatomy, survival increasingly depended on learned skills.

Tracking.

Throwing.

Making better tools.

Using fire.

Cooking.

Building shelters.

Planning tomorrow's hunt.

None of these abilities is fully inherited.

Each must be learned.

As learned abilities become more important, individuals who learn more effectively gain an advantage.

Natural selection is no longer acting only on muscles and bones.

Increasingly, it is rewarding learning itself.

## Why not the winners?

One question remains.

If these adaptations proved so successful, why did every ape lineage not develop them?

Perhaps because they never needed to.

Evolution does not seek the best solution.

It seeks a solution that is good enough.

If the original strategy continues to succeed, there is little pressure to abandon it.

The winners optimize.

The displaced explore.

Occasionally, exploration discovers something optimization never would.

## The evolution of second choices

This is not an argument that losing competition guarantees success.

Most individuals who lose fail.

Most populations under pressure disappear.

Lacking alone creates no miracles.

But when countless individuals confront the same problem across immense spans of time, the probability that a few stumble upon a better solution becomes surprisingly high.

We cannot predict which child will one day become a billionaire.

Yet in a society of hundreds of millions of people, we are unsurprised that a few eventually do.

Evolution plays a similar game, only with vastly larger numbers and vastly longer timescales.

Perhaps our ancestors did not become extraordinary because they possessed extraordinary bodies.

Perhaps they became extraordinary because ordinary solutions stopped working.

Forced to search for second choices, they gradually discovered that the most flexible tool they carried was not their hands, nor stones, nor fire.

It was the ability to learn.

If so, then human intelligence may not have begun with superiority.

It may have begun with necessity.
